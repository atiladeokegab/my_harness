# Show where to start

Source: Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think!*, 2nd edition (New Riders, 2006), ch. 7 "The first step in
recovery is admitting that the Home page is beyond your control". Paraphrased in our words; anything
quoted is marked and at most one sentence.

**Principle.** Once the visitor knows what this is, the first screen must make the way in obvious: the
entry to the main task looks like an entry and is labelled as one.

## Why it holds

- Ch. 7 calls this the fifth question. After a quick look, a visitor should know where to start to
  search, to browse, and to sample the best of what is there. In a step-by-step product the start of
  the process should leap out; if there are accounts, sign-up and sign-in should be easy to find.
- Promos that each shout "start here" bury the real entry points. The cure is to make each entry point
  look like its kind (a search box like a search box, a list of sections like a list) and to label it
  in plain words: Search, Browse by category, Sign in, Start here.
- Krug's eTour case: one big, generic "Let's Go!" button worked for first visits but caught him again
  on his second, third and fourth, when he should have signed in. Make clear who each entry is for.
  Their redesign renamed it "Sign Up" and added a sign-in box.
- Promoting everything is the tragedy of the commons again: each addition helps its owner and weakens
  the screen for everyone.

## What to check on a screen

- One control is visibly the primary action, and it is above the fold.
- Its label says what it starts, not a generic go-word.
- A returning user's way in (sign in, open recent) is present and quieter than the primary action.

## Pass or fail test

Apply to each capture on its own: desktop 1280x633 and phone 390x757. Point to the control you would
use first for the product's main job.

- **PASS:** exactly one control is styled as the primary action; it is fully above the fold; its label
  names what it starts; if the product has accounts, a sign-in entry is visible and less prominent.
- **FAIL:** no primary control, several competing ones, one below the fold, or a label that does not
  say what it starts.

Observable: the primary-styled control or controls, with their labels and positions.
