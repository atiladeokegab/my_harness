# Self-evident, or at least self-explanatory

Source: Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think!*, 2nd edition (New Riders, 2006), ch. 1 "Don't make me
think!" and ch. 2. Paraphrased in our words; anything quoted is marked and at most one sentence.

**Principle.** A first-time user should see what a screen is and how to use it without spending
thought on it; only a genuinely new idea may cost a little thought, never more.

## Why it holds

- Ch. 1, Krug's first law. Every question a screen raises (can I click that, why is it called that,
  are these two links the same thing) adds to the visitor's load and pulls attention off their task.
  Each one is tiny; together they wear people out.
- Few people leave over one puzzle, and many blame themselves. The real cost is that the product feels
  like effort, and a team that did not bother to make things obvious loses the visitor's confidence.
- Ch. 2 sets the bar: people give a screen far less time than its makers imagine, so it has to work at
  a glance.
- The usual culprits: clever, marketing or in-house names; controls whose clickability is unclear; a
  familiar word dressed up ("Quick Search" for Search); asking users how the system should treat their
  input (a search that wants Title, Author or Keyword chosen first, against one that takes the words).

## What to check on a screen

- Every label names the thing in the user's words, not the team's.
- Nothing on the main path needs a tooltip, help link or tutorial to be understood.
- The system takes input as typed instead of asking the user how to phrase it.
- Where the idea is new, a few well-chosen words sit beside the thing itself, not on a help page.

## Pass or fail test

Apply to each capture on its own: desktop 1280x633 and phone 390x757. Use only what the capture shows.
1. Name the screen's main job from its heading and its most prominent control.
2. List each visible element a first-time user needs for that job.
3. For each, answer: what is it, can I act on it, what happens if I do. Mark any answer that is a guess.

- **PASS:** no guesses on the main-job elements.
- **FAIL:** one or more; name each element and the question it leaves open.

Observable: the main-job elements, each with the question it raises (or none).
