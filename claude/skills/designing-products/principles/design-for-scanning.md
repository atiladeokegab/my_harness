# Design for scanning, satisficing and muddling through

Source: Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think!*, 2nd edition (New Riders, 2006), ch. 2 "How we really use
the Web" and ch. 3. Paraphrased in our words; anything quoted is marked and at most one sentence.

**Principle.** Build for people who glance, take the first option that looks right and never learn how
the thing works, not for readers who weigh every choice.

## Why it holds

- Ch. 2 names three habits seen in years of watching people:
  - **They scan, not read.** They are in a hurry, only a sliver of the screen matters to their task,
    and they have skimmed print all their lives. They latch on to words that match their task.
  - **They satisfice.** They take the first reasonable option, not the best one. Krug borrows Gary
    Klein's study of fire commanders, who acted on the first workable plan. Online a wrong guess costs
    one Back, weighing options rarely helps, and guessing is quicker.
  - **They muddle through.** Almost nobody reads instructions. People invent a plausible story of how
    the thing works and stick with whatever works for them, however clumsy.
- Those who do "get it" find more, see more of what is offered, feel capable and come back.
- Krug's conclusion: if the audience treats screens like roadside billboards, make great billboards.

## What to check on a screen

- The words that match the user's task come first in headings, labels and links.
- The first plausible-looking option on the main path is the right one, so a satisficer lands well.
- No step depends on reading a paragraph.
- The main path works for someone who never forms the right mental model.
