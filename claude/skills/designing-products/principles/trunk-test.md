# The trunk test

Source: Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think!*, 2nd edition (New Riders, 2006), ch. 6 "Street signs and
Breadcrumbs". Paraphrased in our words; anything quoted is marked and at most one sentence.

**Principle.** Dropped onto any screen deep in the product, a stranger should see at once which product
it is, which screen this is, what the main sections are, what they can do here, where they are, and how
to search.

## Why it holds

- Ch. 6: on screen there is no sense of scale, direction or place, and people often arrive mid-product
  from a search or a shared link, never having seen the start. Navigation supplies the missing "there":
  it shows what exists, how to use the product, and that its makers know what they are doing.
- Persistent navigation stays in one place and looks the same everywhere, so it is learnt once and
  confirms you are still in the same product. A form or checkout may trim it to identity and a way back.
- Every screen needs a name that frames its own content, usually as the largest text; a highlighted
  menu item is not a substitute.
- "You are here" markers most often fail by being too subtle. Use more than one distinction (colour
  and weight, say); Krug's advice is that if it already feels too loud to the designer, double it.
- Lower levels get as little care as the top and fall apart; plan them before polishing the first screen.
- Krug's trunk: imagine being driven blindfolded and dumped on a random page, eyes still blurry, so the
  elements must stand out by appearance alone, not on close reading.

## What to check on a screen

Product identity at the top; a screen name; the sections; local options; a visible marker on the
current section; search wherever there is content to search; the same names in the same order on
every screen.

## Pass or fail test

Apply to each capture on its own: desktop 1280x633 and phone 390x757. Pick a screen that is not the
first screen. Judge by shape and position as if squinting; do not read small text. Find: (1) identity,
(2) screen name, (3) main sections (on phone, the visible control that opens them: our adaptation),
(4) local options, (5) a "you are here" marker, (6) search.

- **PASS:** 1, 2, 3 and 5 found; 4 and 6 found wherever they apply; the marker differs from its
  siblings in at least two ways. A form or checkout screen needs only 1, 2 and a way back.
- **FAIL:** name each missing item, or the marker that differs in only one way.

Observable: the six items, each marked found, missing or not applicable.

## Used at

**Critique** (run on a deep screen, never only the first). The UX step designs navigation for every
level.
