# Omit needless words

Source: Steve Krug, *Don't Make Me Think!*, 2nd edition (New Riders, 2006), ch. 5 "Omit needless
words", with ch. 7. Paraphrased in our words; anything quoted is marked and at most one sentence.

**Principle.** Cut the words nobody will read (welcome talk and instructions first), then cut again.

## Why it holds

- Ch. 5, Krug's third law, built on Strunk and White's rule: "Get rid of half the words on each page,
  then get rid of half of what's left." Krug says halving is realistic on most screens; the second
  half is there to make people ruthless.
- Extra words make a screen look as if it must be read, so it seems harder than it is. Cutting them
  lowers the noise, lifts the useful content and brings more of the screen above the fold.
- **Happy talk** is welcome text and self-praise that carries no fact. Krug's test: while reading it
  you can hear a voice in your head going blah blah blah. It thrives on section front pages that have
  nothing of their own.
- **Instructions** go unread until muddling through has failed. Aim to make them unnecessary; where one
  is needed, cut it to the minimum and put it where it is acted on. Krug's survey example went from
  103 words to 41 by keeping only what helped the decision at that moment.
- Ch. 7: a corporate mission statement is not a welcome blurb; nobody reads it.

## What to check on a screen

- No "Welcome to...", and no praise without a concrete fact behind it.
- No directions for using standard controls (how to fill a form, what a dropdown is).
- Any necessary note sits next to the control it is about, at the moment it matters.
- Every sentence would be missed if it were deleted.

## Pass or fail test

Apply to each capture on its own: desktop 1280x633 and phone 390x757. Read every sentence of running
text (not labels or navigation). Mark a sentence if it could describe any product, praises without a
fact, or explains something the screen itself could make obvious.

- **PASS:** no sentence marked.
- **FAIL:** quote each marked sentence and say which kind it is (happy talk or instruction).

Observable: the marked sentences, or "none".
